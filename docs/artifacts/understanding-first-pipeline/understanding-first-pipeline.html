Understanding-First Pipeline
Build Studio · architecture proposal v5 · not built

Two tracks in, one workflow out

A recording is processed along two tracks at the same time. Track A shrinks the video, cuts its idle stretches, blurs what was typed, marks every click, and sends it to Pegasus once. Pegasus says what happened, why, and what the screen looked like. Track B processes the browser's own signals: exact clicks, elements, timings and page changes. The two are lined up in order on one clock, and one model call turns the result into an understanding of the task. It decides most open points by itself and asks you only what you alone know or must approve. Then come compile and the test. Studio no longer cuts frames, stores screenshots in the skill package, or asks a model to write each step's intent.

The architecture

  • Deterministic code
  • Pegasus video model
  • Language model, checked in code
  • You decide
  • Loop back on a failed test

The two tracks

Both tracks start as soon as you stop recording. Track B finishes in seconds on your machine. Track A waits on Pegasus, so the merge starts once both are done.

Track A · video
  1. A1 Prepare video. Stitch the tab videos into one, cut idle stretches, shrink it, blur typed values and draw a ring at each click. All of it is driven by the recorder's own event times and positions, and every cut and join goes in the splice map. Details below.
  2. A2 Pegasus watches. First look in the cache. On a miss, upload through the Conxa cloud, which holds the TwelveLabs key, meters the call, and deletes the uploaded video afterwards. One async request runs on it: time-based segments. There is no separate walkthrough request, because the understanding call writes both summaries.
  3. A3 Video signals. Code reads the segments into rows: time range, action kind, visible target, intent, purpose, screen before, screen after, and whether it happened outside the page.
Track B · browser
  1. B1 Normalize. The existing event pipeline: normalize, dedupe, enrich the recorded events.
  2. B2 Clean and fold. Existing compiler preparation: drop noise, fold a redirect into the click that caused it, add tab-open and tab-switch markers.
  3. B3 Browser signals. For each event: the element's IdentityBundle (role, test id, text, relations, stable hash), its frame and tab chain, the typed or chosen value, page changes, downloads, dialogs, popups, and the exact millisecond time. No model is involved.
A1 · preparing the video
  1. Stitch. Join the tab videos in the order they happened, using whichever tab was in front. Scale tabs recorded at different sizes to one size. Pad a video under 4 seconds by holding its last frame.
  2. Cut idle time. Any stretch over 5 seconds with no browser event keeps its first and last second, so Pegasus still sees the spinner. The middle is cut, and the splice map records where and how long.
  3. Shrink. Re-encode at 720p and 10 frames a second. That is enough to read buttons and text, and far less to upload. Check TwelveLabs' minimum resolution and frame rate before building.
  4. Blur. Password fields are always blurred, from the first keystroke until the page changes. A company setting, "blur all typed values", does the same for every field typed into. The boxes come from the recorder's page-level positions, padded by 8 px.
  5. Click rings. Draw a ring for 0.6 seconds at each click's recorded position and time. Video models notice a click far more reliably when they can see it. The rings go into the copy sent to Pegasus only, never into the page during recording, so page snapshots stay clean.
  6. Fingerprint. The SHA-256 of the prepared video is the cache key. Pegasus's result is saved beside the session. A recompile, an edit or a re-answered question reuses it. Only a new recording, or a change to the blur setting, calls Pegasus again.

M: merging the tracks

The merge is plain code. It decides nothing about meaning. It only puts each video row next to the browser event it describes.

  1. One clock. A Pegasus time on the combined video goes back through the splice map to a tab and a wall-clock time. A browser event's time is its tab video's start_wall_ms plus its timestamp_ms.
  2. Cuts become waits. Each idle stretch cut in A1 becomes an off-page observation: a long wait, with its real length from the splice map.
  3. Split at tab switches. A segment that runs across a tab switch is split there, so each half belongs to one tab.
  4. Line up in order. Both lists already run in time order, so they are lined up the way a diff tool lines up two files: one pass that never reorders either list. Pairing an event with a segment costs more the further the event sits outside the segment, with 1 second of slack because Pegasus rounds to whole seconds, and more again when action_kind differs. Leaving an event or a segment unpaired has a fixed cost. One segment may take several events, such as typing and then pressing Enter. The lowest total cost wins. Two clicks in the same second can't swap, because order is never broken.
  5. Off-page observation. A segment with no event is kept as an observation, such as a native dialog, the download bar or a short wait.
  6. Pegasus missed it. An event with no segment is kept and flagged. The browser always wins, because it is the record of what actually happened.
  7. Values seen in both. A typed or clicked value that also appears in Pegasus's target text is marked, which helps the model decide whether it is fixed or changes each run.

U: the one model call

One text call reads the merged timeline and returns an understanding that code checks before anyone sees it. It never sees or writes a selector.

Takes in
  • The merged timeline: each event with its matched segment, plus off-page observations and missed events
  • Anything you said in the chat before, during or after recording
  • Candidate values: page addresses, clicked items and typed text
  • What is already known about this app from earlier answers
  • Moments code flagged as must-ask, and the rules of asking
Puts out · checked in code
  • Goal, and the phases of the task
  • For each candidate value: fixed, changes each run, or unsure, and a name for it as an input
  • How success shows at the end
  • Gaps: anything Pegasus saw that the browser did not record, or the other way round
  • A short summary and a detailed summary. This is the only place summaries are written.
  • A list of points it decided, each with its reason, and a short list of questions it could not decide, both tied to rows of the timeline

How questions are made

Studio holds no list of questions. The model reads a briefing, decides everything it can by itself, and asks only what it can't. Code checks what it returns. You answer only two kinds of thing: what only you know, and what you must approve.

LayerDoesIn this pipeline
CodeThe routine work. Same result every time, free, no thinking.Recording, merging, compiling, replaying steps, flagging risky moments
ModelThe thinking. Used only where meaning is unclear.Working out the task, deciding most open points, wording the few questions
YouOnly what no machine can stand in for.Facts only you know, and approving steps that can't be undone
The briefing · what the model reads
  1. What happened. The merged timeline. Each step has the browser's facts (element, value, page change, time) and Pegasus's meaning (intent, screen before and after).
  2. What you said. Anything typed in the chat before, during or after recording, such as "do this for every new invoice".
  3. What is already known. Answers you gave on earlier workflows for the same app, such as a popup you said to always close. Anything settled there is not asked again.
  4. What code flagged. Moments in the browser signals that always need a person: a click that deletes, overwrites, sends or pays, and any popup that asks for a decision. The model words these questions. It can't drop them.
  5. The rules of asking. How to choose between deciding and asking, and how to word a question. Not the questions themselves.
The rules of asking · for each open point
  1. Can I see the answer? In the recording, in what you said, or in what is already known. If yes, decide it and note the reason.
  2. Does a wrong guess break a future run? If no, as with an input's name, decide it.
  3. Is it something only you know, or must approve? If yes, ask. If no, decide it and mark it "worth a look".

How a question is worded

  • One plain sentence about your task, never about how Studio works
  • Points to the moment in the recording it is about
  • Two or three choices plus "something else", and one line on why it matters
  • No suggested answer when the question is an approval
What to look for · a lens, not a script

The model checks the recording for these kinds of open points and writes its own wording for whatever it finds. A recording with none of them gets no questions.

  • Values that may change each run: a typed number, a picked item, a page address
  • Where the task ends, and what on screen shows it worked
  • Parts that happened only this time, such as a popup or a one-off detour
  • Cases the recording didn't show, such as nothing found, or an item already done
  • Anything missing: work done outside the browser, or a step that seems skipped
  • Files going in or coming out
  • Steps that can't be undone
Example · decided by the model
  • INV-20931 changes each run, as the input "invoice number". You typed it into a search box, and the chat said "every new invoice".
  • It worked when the PDF downloads. The recording ends on the download.
  • The cookie banner is always closed. You already said so on another workflow for this app.

What code checks before you see it

  1. Every decision and question points to a real moment in the timeline. Anything else is dropped.
  2. A moment code flagged as must-ask is put back if the model left it out.
  3. A point already settled for this app is dropped.
  4. At most five questions show, ranked by how much a wrong guess would cost and how unsure the model is. The rest sit under "More". A question left closed keeps the recording as it is.
  5. The model's decisions apply only when you press "Looks good". Nothing changes behind your back.
  6. If the call fails, nothing blocks. You see the summary and can go on with the recording as it is.

Thinking once is enough

Every answer is saved for the app. The next workflow on the same app starts with it in the briefing, so a point settled once is not asked again. Saved answers describe how the app behaves, such as "this popup is always closed", and never hold the values you typed. Over time questions get fewer: what needed you becomes the model's job, and what needed the model becomes routine.

Limits to plan around

Pegasus timing is coarse

Whole seconds, in segments at least about 2 seconds long. The merge keeps the browser's order and uses Pegasus only for meaning, so coarse times can leave an event unmatched but can't swap two events.

Every recording leaves the machine

The prepared video goes to TwelveLabs through the Conxa cloud and is deleted after analysis. Passwords are blurred, and typed values too when the company turns that on, but the rest of the screen is sent as is. TwelveLabs is still a new processor of customer data, which needs an entry in the security gap tracker and a line in customer terms.

Blur follows recorded positions

A field that moves while it holds a value, through a scroll or an animation, can slip past its padded box. Check every trial recording for leaks before trusting the blur.

Pegasus becomes a hard dependency

With no fallback path, nothing past the merge can start while TwelveLabs is down or slow. Plan for retries and a clear "still watching your recording" state.

Cost and wait

The v3 estimate for two requests was about $0.06–0.10 per recorded minute. One request on a shorter, smaller video should cost well under that, and a cache hit costs nothing. Neither the new price nor the async wait has been measured.

Text instead of pictures in recovery

The recovery agent loses its reference image. Check in the trial whether stuck runs on icon-only or visually similar elements recover as often with words alone.

Prove it first

Run a 5-recording trial. Measure how many events Pegasus matches, how good its intent is next to today's, how many useful off-page moments it finds, how stuck runs recover without screenshots, whether 720p at 10 frames a second loses anything, whether the blur leaks, and how many questions people answer before pressing Confirm.
